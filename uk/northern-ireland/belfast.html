Belfast —
capital city where industrial heritage, political history and contemporary culture converge

Belfast is the capital of Northern Ireland — a city with profound recent history, remarkable industrial heritage and genuine contemporary energy. The Titanic was built in Belfast's Harland & Wolff shipyard. The city's architecture reflects centuries of shipbuilding wealth and urban development. Belfast's recent political history is visible in peace walls and political murals — expressions of community identity and the city's journey through conflict towards peace. The city has changed significantly in recent decades and remains complex. We visited Belfast, took a Black Cab political tour through the Falls Road and Shankill Road areas to see murals and understand community divisions, and drove around Northern Ireland ourselves. Belfast offers far more context when its industrial, cultural and political history are considered together.

Belfast's history and context

From settlement to industrial powerhouse to contemporary city

Belfast's history spans centuries but the city's transformation as an industrial powerhouse came in the 19th century. The development of the shipbuilding industry — particularly Harland & Wolff — made Belfast one of the world's major industrial cities. Ships were built here and shipped worldwide. This wealth funded Victorian and Edwardian development that characterises much of central Belfast's architecture.

The 20th century brought profound change. Northern Ireland's partition in 1921 made Belfast the capital of a new political entity. The subsequent decades saw industrial decline as shipbuilding moved elsewhere. More significantly, from the late 1960s onwards, Belfast became the focal point of "the Troubles" — a period of violent conflict between nationalist/republican and unionist/loyalist communities, with complex political dimensions reflecting broader Irish history. The conflict lasted roughly three decades until the Good Friday Agreement in 1998 brought a peace settlement.

Contemporary Belfast is a city in transition — moving beyond conflict, developing economically, engaging with difficult history, building shared cultural spaces. The process is ongoing and imperfect but genuinely happening.

Black Cab political tours

Understanding Belfast through community perspectives

Black Cab tours are a distinctive Belfast experience. Local taxi drivers, often with personal connections to communities affected by the Troubles, drive small groups through areas like Falls Road and Shankill Road, explaining history, pointing out murals, and providing perspective on political divisions and community identity. The tours help visitors understand Belfast's complexity in ways that academic reading cannot.

We took a Black Cab political tour through the Falls Road and Shankill Road areas. Seeing the murals while hearing the historical context gave us a clearer sense of how community identity and recent history are reflected in the city. Individual tours and guides vary, so the exact route and emphasis can differ.

For visitors interested in Belfast's recent history, these tours can provide useful context when approached respectfully and with an awareness that perspectives can differ.

Falls Road, Shankill Road and political murals

Murals, walls and visible history

The Falls Road (predominantly nationalist/republican) and Shankill Road (predominantly unionist/loyalist) neighbourhoods are where Belfast's political murals are most visible and concentrated. Murals depict political figures, commemorate historical events, express community identity and sometimes memorialize individuals killed during the Troubles. The murals are public art expressing community values and historical memory.

Peace walls — barriers erected to separate communities — remain in some locations. These physical structures are tangible evidence of division. Some walls have been partly dismantled; others remain. Some barriers remain, while wider efforts around shared space and reconciliation continue.

Visiting these areas is most meaningful through guided tours where drivers/guides can explain context, point out specific murals and their significance, and help visitors understand the complexity beyond visual appearance. Black Cab tours provide this context naturally.

Titanic and shipbuilding heritage

Belfast built the Titanic

The Titanic was built in Belfast's Harland & Wolff shipyard between 1909 and 1911. The shipyard was one of the world's largest and most advanced, capable of building massive ocean liners. The Titanic's construction was a massive undertaking — thousands of workers, cutting-edge technology, and resources.

The ship's sinking in 1912 remains the most famous maritime disaster. The connection to Belfast gives the city prominence in Titanic history. Harland & Wolff's cranes remain iconic elements of Belfast's skyline — massive industrial structures that dominate the city's approach from the water and represent the city's industrial past.

Belfast's shipbuilding story

Beyond the Titanic, Belfast's shipbuilding industry built thousands of vessels. The industry was central to Belfast's economy and identity for over a century. As shipbuilding declined, Belfast's economy and identity shifted. The visible remnants — the shipyard landscape, the cranes, the industrial buildings — are now heritage and tourism attractions rather than active industrial sites.

Understanding Belfast's Titanic connection and broader shipbuilding heritage adds depth to visiting the city. The industrial architecture and landscape tell stories of a city built on making ships.

Belfast City Hall

Civic centre and architectural landmark

Belfast City Hall sits in Donegall Square in the city centre — an ornate Edwardian Baroque building that represents civic pride and urban importance. Built in 1906, the building is architecturally impressive and remains the seat of Belfast City Council. The building and square represent Belfast's prosperous Edwardian period when the city was wealthy and confident from industrial success.

The City Hall has exhibition spaces that sometimes display Belfast-focused exhibitions. The exterior is worth seeing and photographing. The square itself is a pleasant public space with green areas and surrounding architecture.

Cathedral Quarter and cultural district

Arts, restaurants and nightlife

The Cathedral Quarter (around St Anne's Cathedral) has become Belfast's primary arts and restaurant district. Street art, galleries, independent shops, restaurants and bars have transformed what was previously a neglected area. The neighbourhood represents contemporary Belfast — young, creative, forward-looking. It's where much of Belfast's contemporary cultural energy concentrates.

The Cathedral Quarter is worth walking through to experience modern Belfast culture and where to find food, drink and entertainment. The street art reflects contemporary artistic expression rather than political messaging.

St George's Market

Historic market and local experience

St George's Market is a Victorian covered market building in the city centre. The market hosts food and craft vendors, particularly on weekends. It's an active community space where locals shop alongside tourists. The building is architecturally interesting and the market atmosphere is genuine — not a tourist museum but an actual local gathering place.

Museums and cultural attractions

Telling Belfast's stories

Ulster Museum: Comprehensive museum covering natural history, art and cultural history with strong focus on Irish and Northern Irish content. Well-curated and genuinely interesting for understanding the region.

Crumlin Road Gaol: Historic 19th century prison that has been preserved as a museum. Tours explain the building's history and role in Irish political history. The building itself is architecturally striking.

Titanic Belfast: Museum dedicated to the Titanic and Belfast's shipbuilding heritage. Architecturally distinctive building with exhibitions exploring the ship's construction, the disaster and the broader shipbuilding industry.

St Anne's Cathedral: Anglican cathedral with architectural interest and historical significance.

Belfast Castle: Victorian castle overlooking the city with gardens and views. While not a medieval fortress, the building and location offer perspective on the city.

Botanic Gardens: Victorian gardens in South Belfast with greenhouses and pleasant walking. Active community space for locals.

Suggested Belfast itineraries

One day in Belfast

Morning: Start in central Belfast. Visit the City Hall area and Donegall Square. Walk through some of the city centre to get oriented.

Midday: Take a Black Cab political tour through Falls Road and Shankill Road to understand Belfast's political geography and community identities. This is the most distinctive Belfast experience.

Afternoon: Visit Cathedral Quarter to explore contemporary Belfast — restaurants, street art, independent shops and galleries. Have lunch or coffee in this area.

Evening: Walk along the River Lagan waterfront or visit a market if timing aligns. Have dinner in Cathedral Quarter or another neighbourhood restaurant.

Two or three days in Belfast

With more time, you can explore museums (Ulster Museum, Crumlin Road Gaol, Titanic Belfast), visit St George's Market, explore Botanic Gardens, walk more extensively through different neighbourhoods, experience Belfast's restaurant and bar culture more thoroughly, and take time to absorb the city's atmosphere. A longer stay lets you understand Belfast more deeply as an actual city where people live and work rather than just hitting major attractions.

How much time to spend in Belfast

Minimum: A half-day works if you're doing a Black Cab tour and walking the city centre. It's rushed but captures the main distinctive Belfast experience.

Ideal: One full day in Belfast lets you take a Black Cab tour, explore the city centre and Cathedral Quarter, visit at least one major attraction, and experience Belfast without constant rushing. A day feels satisfying.

Extended: Two or three nights in Belfast lets you explore thoroughly, visit multiple museums, experience different neighbourhoods, visit St George's Market, walk green spaces, and engage with Belfast's cultural scene more deeply.

Best time to visit Belfast

Belfast by season

Spring (March-May): Pleasant weather, flowers blooming, fewer crowds than summer. Good time with comfortable temperatures and spring atmosphere.

Summer (June-August): Typically the warmest and one of the busier periods, with more events and visitors. Booking accommodation and major attractions ahead can be useful.

Autumn (September-October): Can be a pleasant time for a city break, with cooler temperatures and fewer summer crowds.

Winter (November-February): Cooler, darker and often wetter, but generally quieter outside major events. Check seasonal opening times before travelling.

Getting to Belfast

Transport to Belfast

By train: Belfast Grand Central Station is the city's main rail and bus hub. Rail services connect Belfast with Dublin and destinations across Northern Ireland. Check current Translink and Irish Rail schedules for journey times and service details.

By bus: Coach services connect Belfast with Dublin and other destinations. Journey times and fares vary, so check current operators before travelling.

By plane: Belfast International Airport and George Best Belfast City Airport both serve the city. Routes change over time, so check current destinations and ground-transport options before booking.

By car: Belfast is straightforward to reach by road from elsewhere on the island of Ireland. City-centre parking can be limited or costly, while having a car is useful if you plan to explore beyond Belfast.

Getting around Belfast

Transport within the city

On foot: Central Belfast is walkable. Main attractions like City Hall, Cathedral Quarter and the city centre are within reasonable walking distance. The River Lagan waterfront is accessible via walking routes.

Buses: Belfast has a comprehensive bus network. Buses are efficient and reasonably priced. They're useful for reaching areas beyond easy walking distance or for hop-on/hop-off convenience.

Black Cab taxis: Regular taxis are available for point-to-point travel. Black Cab tours are distinctive and recommended for understanding the city's political geography.

Walking tours: Various walking tour companies operate in Belfast covering city history, Cathedral Quarter, murals and other themes. Good way to explore with local knowledge.

Cycling: Belfast has some bike paths and is reasonably cycle-friendly in parts. Bike hire is available if you want to explore by bicycle.

Choosing where to stay

City centre: Most convenient for attractions and nightlife. Walking distance to City Hall, Cathedral Quarter and shopping. Can be busier and noisier.

Cathedral Quarter: Vibrant contemporary area with restaurants, bars and galleries. Pleasant for experiencing modern Belfast. Close enough to city centre.

South Belfast (University area): More residential and quieter. Good for experiencing local Belfast life. Slightly less convenient but good value and character.

Budget: Belfast has accommodation at various price points. Peak season (summer and holidays) books up and prices rise. Off-season offers better value.

Food and drink in Belfast

Eating in Belfast

Irish and Northern Irish cuisine: Restaurants emphasise local ingredients, traditional dishes and contemporary interpretations. Seafood, beef and potato-based dishes are common.

Restaurants and cafes: Belfast has diverse dining from casual cafes to more elaborate restaurants. Cathedral Quarter in particular has numerous restaurants and cafes.

Pubs: Traditional Irish pubs serve real ale and pub food. Good for experiencing local atmosphere and where to hear music and conversation.

Markets: St George's Market has food vendors and is good for casual eating. Independent shops and delis offer quality local products.

Practical information

Weather and what to expect

Belfast weather is typical Irish/Northern Irish — changeable, often grey and potentially rainy, but rarely extreme. Spring and autumn are most pleasant. Summer can be warm. Winter is cold. The waterfront can be windy. Layering and waterproof jackets are wise year-round.

Common questions

Belfast FAQ

Is a Black Cab tour necessary to understand Belfast?
A Black Cab tour can be a useful way to understand Belfast's political history and community geography. The tours provide context that helps you see beyond surface-level tourism. However, you can visit Belfast without a tour if you prefer. The city itself tells stories through architecture and murals.
Is it safe to visit areas like Falls Road and Shankill Road?
Falls Road and Shankill Road are living neighbourhoods, and many visitors see them as part of guided tours. As in any city, conditions can change, so follow local guidance, be respectful when photographing residential areas, and avoid treating communities as attractions.
What's the Titanic connection to Belfast?
The Titanic was built in Belfast's Harland & Wolff shipyard between 1909-1911. The shipyard was one of the world's largest industrial facilities. Belfast built thousands of ships. The Titanic is the most famous. The city's shipbuilding heritage is central to its industrial history and identity.
Can I visit the Causeway Coast from Belfast?
Yes, the Causeway Coast is a popular day trip or longer exploration from Belfast. It's roughly 1-1.5 hours drive from the city centre. We drove ourselves around Northern Ireland and found the combination of Belfast city and Causeway Coast countryside works well together. Read our full Causeway Coast guide.
Is Belfast a day trip or overnight destination?
Belfast works as both. A day trip with a Black Cab tour captures the main distinctive experience. An overnight stay lets you explore more thoroughly, visit multiple museums, experience restaurants and bars, and absorb the city's contemporary culture. Most visitors benefit from at least one night.
