💫 Living in Brussels

A year that changed everything

From study abroad to calling Brussels home.

"Brussels didn't just grow on me — it got completely under my skin. I came to study for a year and it became one of the best years of my life. The city rewards those who stay, who explore, who sit in cafés, who drink the beer, who eat their way through the culture." — Nicola

Arriving in Brussels in 2005 to study, Nicola expected to be there for a year. What she found was a city that grew more interesting the longer she spent in it. Not because Brussels is traditionally beautiful in a postcard sense — it isn't, particularly. But because Brussels has a lived-in depth that reveals itself slowly.

The first time you cross Grand Place it stops you in your tracks. UNESCO World Heritage Site surrounded by ornate Gothic and Baroque guild houses, the magnificent Town Hall, the perfect proportions that somehow make everything else in Europe feel a little less grand. But Grand Place is not just a square — it's a gathering place. Sit at one of the cafés ringing it and you understand why Brussels matters. People come here. They stay. They linger over coffee and beer.

The Atomium is different — a 102-metre-tall model of an iron crystal, built for the 1958 World Expo, quirky and futuristic and completely unlike anything else in Europe. It shouldn't work. Somehow it defines Brussels.

The neighbourhoods

Brussels is not one thing. There's the Grand Place area and the EU quarter. There's the Sablon with galleries and antique shops. There's St-Gilles with its Art Nouveau treasures and lived-in charm. There are neighbourhoods filled with character that invite exploration. Living in Brussels means discovering these layers — understanding where locals actually live, where they drink, where they eat.

The beer culture

Belgium's brewing tradition runs deep, and Brussels is where it lives. The saying goes there are 365 beers brewed in Belgium, one for every day of the year. Abbey beers brewed by monks using centuries-old recipes. Trappist ales from monastic communities. Lambics with wild yeasts and fruit. Strong dark beers. Crisp pilsners. Every evening had something different to discover. Belgian beer culture is recognised by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The food

Brussels waffles — fluffy, golden, often served with chocolate or fruit. Belgian chocolate that deserves its reputation. Frites with proper Belgian mayonnaise. Moules-frites — mussels and fries — a Belgian institution. Eating in Brussels is an event, a ritual, part of understanding the culture.

The café culture

This is where Brussels comes alive. Not rushing through a meal. Settling in. Having a coffee. Sitting with a beer for hours. Watching the city pass by. Connecting with people. This is not tourism — it's how you live in Brussels. The café is where everything happens. Find a café that's full of locals, not tourists. Sit. Stay a while. This is where you experience the real Brussels.

By the end of that year, Brussels was not somewhere Nicola had visited — it was somewhere she had lived. She knew the neighbourhoods. She had favourite cafés. She understood the culture. She could navigate the city in French. And she didn't want to leave.

🏛️ What to see in Brussels

The essential Brussels experiences

🏛️
Grand Place

UNESCO World Heritage Site. One of the most spectacular squares in all of Europe. Go at dawn, at noon, at sunset, at night illuminated.

⚛️
The Atomium

102 metres of retro-futuristic wonder. Built for the 1958 World Expo. Quirky, unforgettable, completely Brussels. €13-15 entry.

🚻
Manneken Pis

Brussels' most famous statue. A small boy statue that's surprisingly charming. Often dressed in costume. Free to see.

🏰
Sablon District

Historic neighbourhood with galleries, antique shops, churches. Beautiful and worth exploring. Saturday market is excellent.

🎨
Art Nouveau

St-Gilles and other neighbourhoods are filled with spectacular Art Nouveau buildings. The architecture is extraordinary and free to admire.

🏛️
Museums & Galleries

Magritte Museum, Belgian Comic Strip Center, Royal Museums of Fine Arts. Brussels has serious cultural depth (€10-15 per museum).

🏨 Where to stay in Brussels

Best neighbourhoods for different travellers

Budget & backpackers: Lower Town (Les Marolles) — hostels €25-45/night, local cafés, authentic Brussels, cheaper than central areas
Mid-range families: Sablon district — charming, safe, walkable to main sights, hotels €70-120/night, good restaurants
Luxury & foodies: St-Gilles or Avenue Louise — art deco architecture, upscale hotels €120-200+/night, fine dining, galleries
Central/tourist focus: Grand Place area — convenient but pricier (€100-150+/night), busy, higher prices at restaurants

Our advice: Choose a neighbourhood with personality (Sablon, St-Gilles, Les Marolles) over Grand Place. You'll spend less, experience more Brussels, and feel like a local rather than a tourist.

🚂 Getting around Brussels

Transport, costs and logistics

Metro/Trams/Buses: Unified system. Single ticket €2.50, 10-ticket card €18. Free within city, unlimited travel
Brussels Card: €28-43 for 24/48 hours. Includes public transport + museum entry. Worth it if doing museums + multiple tram journeys
Walking: The central areas (Grand Place, Sablon, St-Gilles) are walkable. Comfortable shoes essential — lots of cobbles
Taxis/Uber: €7-15 for short journeys. Use registered taxis or Uber — cheaper and safer than negotiating with street taxis
Trains to other Belgium cities: Buy at station or 15min.be online. Bruges: €13-15 (55min), Ghent: €13-15 (40min), Antwerp: €9-12 (45min)

Tip: Buy a 10-ticket card if staying 3+ days. Public transport is reliable and comfortable.

💰 How much does Brussels cost?

Budget breakdown per day

Accommodation (per night)

  • Hostels: €25–45 (dorms)
  • Budget hotels: €60–90
  • Mid-range: €90–150
  • Luxury: €150+

Food (per day per person)

  • Street food/cheap eats: €8–15 (waffles, frites, sandwiches)
  • Casual restaurants: €15–30 (lunch/dinner)
  • Mid-range dinner: €30–50
  • Fine dining: €50+

Drinks

  • Beer in café: €3–5
  • Coffee: €2–3
  • Wine: €4–6

Attractions

  • Atomium: €13–15
  • Museums: €10–15 each
  • Brussels Card (24h): €28 (includes transport + museums)

Budget example (per day)

Budget traveller: €60–80/day (hostel, street food, free walking, one paid attraction). Mid-range: €100–150/day (hotel, good restaurants, museums, transport). Luxury: €200+/day (nice hotel, fine dining, galleries).

🌦️ When to visit Brussels

Best time and what to expect

April–May (Spring): 12–16°C, mild, flowers blooming, fewer crowds than summer. Ideal time to visit
June–August (Summer): 18–23°C, warm, busy, peak tourist season. Good weather but crowded on Grand Place. Book accommodation ahead
September–October (Autumn): 10–15°C, pleasant, golden light, crowds thin. Excellent time — weather good, atmosphere relaxed
November–February (Winter): 2–8°C, cold, rainy, occasional snow. December has Christmas markets (magical). Fewer tourists, atmospheric

Weather note: Belgian weather is changeable year-round. Even in summer, rain is possible. Pack layers and a light rain jacket no matter when you visit. This isn't a deterrent — many Brusselians say the city is more beautiful in rain, when the buildings and wet cobbles glow.

🗺️ Using Brussels as a base

Day trips and multi-city combinations

Brussels is perfectly positioned to explore Belgium. The rail network is excellent, tickets are affordable, and all other major Belgian cities are under 1 hour away.

From Brussels in 1 hour or less:
  • Bruges: 55 minutes (€13-15) — medieval perfection
  • Ghent: 40 minutes (€13-15) — vibrant and independent
  • Antwerp: 45 minutes (€9-12) — Flemish character
  • Liège: 1 hour (€14-17) — Walloon soul

Suggested itineraries using Brussels

3 days: Brussels 2 days (Grand Place, Atomium, cafés) + 1 day trip to Bruges or Ghent.

5 days: Brussels 2 days + day trip to Bruges + day trip to Ghent + 1 day Antwerp or Liège.

7+ days: Brussels 2 days, then move through Belgium — Bruges 2 nights, Ghent 1 night, Antwerp 1 night, Liège 1 night. Or stay in Brussels and day trip each.

Best strategy: Stay in Brussels 2–3 nights to settle in, then either day trip from Brussels or move to 1–2 other cities. Rail connections are seamless.

🗣️ Language in Brussels

What you need to know

Brussels is officially bilingual: French and Dutch (Flemish). In practice, French dominates daily life. Signs are in both languages.

English: Widely spoken in tourism, hotels, restaurants. You'll be fine without French or Dutch
French phrases (appreciated): "Bonjour" (hello), "S'il vous plaît" (please), "Merci" (thank you), "L'addition" (the bill)
Respect tip: Start with French, not English. Trying to speak French (even badly) earns warmth. English-first can feel entitled to some Belgians

Don't stress. The Brussels tourism industry is English-friendly. But effort with French is appreciated and rewarded with friendliness.

Common questions

Brussels FAQ

Can you do a day trip to Brussels from Amsterdam?
Yes. Brussels is about 2 hours by train from Amsterdam. You can day trip, though staying overnight is better to experience the city properly. Direct trains run regularly throughout the day. Buy tickets at the station or online in advance for better prices.
How much does a trip to Brussels cost?
Budget options: €30-60/night hostels, €60-120/night budget hotels, €120+/night mid-range. Meals: €8-15 street food, €15-30 casual restaurants, €40+ fine dining. Beer: €3-5 per drink. Museums: €10-15 entry. Total budget estimate: €60-100/day per person.
What is the best time to visit Brussels?
Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) are ideal — mild weather, fewer crowds. Summer (June–August) is warm but busy. Winter is cold and rainy but atmospheric, especially December with Christmas markets. Belgian weather is changeable year-round, so pack layers. Avoid August if you dislike crowds.
Is Brussels expensive compared to other Belgium cities?
Slightly more expensive than Bruges or Liège, but cheaper than major capitals like Amsterdam or Paris. Tourism in the Markt area is pricey; local neighbourhoods are reasonable. Budget travellers can eat well for €20-30/day; mid-range visitors €50-70/day.
How long should you spend in Brussels?
3–4 days minimum to explore properly. One day sees the main sights (Grand Place, Atomium). Two days adds neighbourhoods and cafés. Three days lets you live like a local, spend time in cafés, and actually absorb the city. A week using Brussels as a base to day trip to other Belgian cities is ideal.
What language is spoken in Brussels?
Officially bilingual: French and Dutch (Flemish). French is more common in daily life. Most people in tourism speak English. Learning basic French phrases is appreciated. Signs are in both languages. Don't worry — English works, but effort with French earns warmth.
Is Brussels safe for tourists?
Yes. Like any major city, be aware in crowded tourist areas and late at night. The Markt and central areas are safe. Use common sense: watch belongings, avoid empty streets at night, use registered taxis or Uber. Locals navigate Brussels freely — tourists generally have no safety issues.
Can you use Brussels as a base to visit other Belgium cities?
Perfect. Bruges and Ghent are under 1 hour by train. Antwerp is 45 minutes. Liège is 1 hour. You can stay in Brussels and day trip anywhere in Belgium, or use it as your starting point and move through the country. The rail network is excellent and tickets are affordable.
